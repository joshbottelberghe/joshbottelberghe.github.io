Dampening: Spending Freshness to Protect an SLA

2026 · systems design, trade-offs

One of a series of design writeups reconstructed from memory: the structures and trade-offs of systems I built, not internal documentation. Specifics are generalized; the engineering ideas are the point.

The recompute-avoidance work made each update cheaper. This is the other half of the same problem, and it is the less comfortable one: some updates are not worth processing at all, and deciding that means deliberately showing the customer something less than the truth.

The shape of the problem

The pipeline carried a freshness target measured in single-digit minutes. At scale the collector became CPU and memory bound, and the pressure was not evenly distributed. A small proportion of prefixes generated a large share of the load, because they were flapping: withdrawn and readvertised over and over, often from a single unstable link or a misbehaving device somewhere in a customer's network.

Every one of those transitions is a real routing event, and the naive pipeline treats it as one. Each gets ingested, computed, cached, and served. So a handful of unstable prefixes can consume a disproportionate share of the compute budget, and the cost lands on everyone: the freshness target slips for every route and every tenant, including the overwhelming majority that are perfectly stable.

Making the pipeline faster does not fix this. The work is genuinely being requested. The question is whether all of it is worth doing.

The mechanism

Dampening borrows the idea from BGP route flap damping: put a penalty on a prefix each time it changes, decay that penalty on a half-life, and once it crosses a threshold, stop propagating updates for that prefix until it settles. Suppressed prefixes stop being recomputed. The churn stops at the edge of the pipeline instead of travelling all the way through it.

I wrote the algorithm, tuned the penalty and half-life against real traffic analysis rather than guesses, and put the suppression state behind metrics so it was observable in production. The production window was later widened based on what the data actually showed, not on the original estimate.

The part worth being honest about

This has a cost, the customer sees it, and no amount of further engineering removes it. While a prefix is suppressed, that customer's view of it is stale. In a system whose entire purpose is showing customers their routes, I chose to knowingly not show some of them.

That is the trade, stated plainly: unbounded local freshness in exchange for bounded global freshness. The alternative is worse. Letting churn from a few unstable prefixes eat the budget pushes every other route past the target, which is a larger failure spread across every tenant rather than a smaller one confined to the prefixes that are actually broken.

What I could control was whether the staleness was silent. Silent staleness would not have been acceptable, because a customer looking at a stale route with no indication has been given wrong information. Visible staleness is a different thing entirely. So the suppressed state was made legible and self-healing:

  • Suppressed prefixes stay in the view, marked as suppressed, rather than quietly vanishing or silently going stale.
  • An unsuppress poll restores them as soon as they stabilize, so recovery does not wait on the next unrelated event.
  • The dampening state survives restarts, so a deploy does not reset every penalty to zero and immediately re-enter the churn storm it was protecting against.
  • Suppression is instrumented, so it can be watched rather than assumed.

It was rolled out gradually and only enabled by default once the parameters held up in production.

The part I did not expect

The suppression list turned out to be useful on its own.

A suppressed prefix is, by definition, a prefix that is misbehaving. So the list of what is currently dampened is a direct signal about which parts of a network are unstable, and it reads as a diagnostic rather than as an artifact of our rate limiting. Dampened-route analysis became a normal starting point when investigating a customer's routing problems.

That does not give back the freshness that was traded away, and it would be revisionist to claim the trade was free. But the machinery built to be honest about the cost turned into something people wanted for its own sake. The throttle became a diagnostic.

What I'd pull out of it

  • Not all requested work is worth doing. The instinct under load is to process the same queue faster. Sometimes the right move is to decide that part of the queue does not deserve the compute, which is a product decision wearing a performance costume.
  • Protecting the aggregate means failing something specific. Bounded global freshness is bought with unbounded local staleness. Naming which one you are choosing beats pretending you got both.
  • Where a cost cannot be removed, make it observable and reversible. Silent degradation is the unacceptable version of this design. Visible degradation, with a defined path back, is a feature.
  • Instability is itself information. The mechanism that suppresses a bad signal also identifies it, and that identification can be worth more to the user than the suppressed data was.
