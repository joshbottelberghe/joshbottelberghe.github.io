Recompute Avoidance: Scaling a Route-Visualization Pipeline ~3x

2026 · systems design, scaling

One of a series of design writeups reconstructed from memory: the structures and trade-offs of systems I built, not internal documentation. Specifics are generalized; the engineering ideas are the point.

I spent a chunk of my time at a multi-cloud networking startup owning the route-visualization pipeline, the system that takes the live routing state off a fleet of network nodes and turns it into something a customer can actually look at and reason about: which prefixes are reachable, via which path, and why. At scale that means hundreds of tenants, tens of thousands of routes each, and a freshness target measured in single-digit minutes. The interesting work wasn't the visualization. It was keeping the pipeline that feeds it from falling over as the customer base grew.

This is the story of a roughly 3x throughput improvement, and why the win came from doing less work, not from doing the same work faster. The approach was novel enough that we filed a patent on it.

The shape of the problem

The pipeline is conceptually simple: routes land from the nodes into durable object storage, a compute layer reads them, transforms them into the visualized model, and serves that through a cache to customer-facing APIs.

nodes → object store → cache-compute layer → customer APIs

The naive version recomputes the world. A tenant's routing state changes, a handful of prefixes move, and the pipeline rebuilds that tenant's entire visualized model from scratch: re-reads the full route set, re-derives every path, re-serializes everything into the cache. That's fine for ten tenants. At hundreds, with churn happening constantly, you're burning CPU recomputing enormous models in response to tiny deltas, and the freshness SLA starts slipping because the compute layer can't keep up with the change rate.

The instinct, mine included early on, is to make the recompute faster: profile the hot path, tighten the serialization, throw more cores at it. That buys you a constant factor and then you're back in the same place one growth cycle later, because the cost is structurally tied to total state, not to how much actually changed.

The reframe: avoid the recompute

The lever that actually mattered was changing when compute happens at all. Three pieces, each a variation on the same idea: spend work in proportion to the change, not the size of the world.

1. Segment-level cache compute. Instead of treating a tenant's model as one monolithic artifact, partition it along a natural boundary in the domain (here, routing segments). A change touches one segment; only that segment's slice of the model gets recomputed and rewritten. The blast radius of an update drops from "the whole tenant" to "the part that moved." This is the single biggest win, and it's almost entirely a data-modeling decision, not a performance hack.

2. Route hinting. The pipeline doesn't have to rediscover what changed by diffing large states; the layer producing the routes already knows. Carry that signal forward as a hint, so the compute layer can skip straight to the affected work instead of scanning to find it. Cheap to produce, expensive to live without: it's the difference between "tell me what changed" and "figure out what changed."

3. A file-based export worker pool. Export, serializing the computed model out to where it's served, was its own bottleneck and didn't need to share fate with compute. Decoupling it into a pool of workers writing file-based artifacts let export scale horizontally and absorb bursts without back-pressuring the compute stage. Producer/consumer separation, applied to the part of the pipeline that was quietly serializing everything behind a lock.

Why it held up

Together these took the pipeline from "recompute the world on every change" to "do work proportional to the delta," which is what let it carry several times the load on the same footprint while holding the freshness target: hundreds of tenants, no major outages in the domain over the period I owned it.

The part I find most worth repeating is the diagnostic one. Much later, when I built a production-replay harness to soak-test changes against real recorded traffic, the data confirmed the original bet: the dominant cost was cold recompute, and the highest-value remaining levers were all forms of recompute avoidance (content hashing to gate redundant work, dependency tracking so a change invalidates only what truly depends on it), not faster access to the inputs. The first optimization and the measurement years later were the same idea seen twice.

What I'd pull out of it

  • At scale, the question is rarely "how do I do this faster," it's "how do I not do this." Constant-factor speedups are a treadmill when the cost scales with total state. Restructuring so cost scales with change is what actually moves the curve.
  • A lot of "performance work" is data-modeling work in disguise. The biggest gain here was choosing the right partition boundary, which is a modeling decision, not a profiling one.
  • Propagate what the system already knows. The producer knew what changed; making the consumer rediscover it was wasted effort. Hints beat diffs.
  • Measure to confirm the shape, not just the number. The soak harness mattered less for the throughput figure than for proving the cost was where I thought it was, which is what tells you the next lever is avoidance, not acceleration.
